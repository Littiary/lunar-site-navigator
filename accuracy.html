Lunar Site Navigator: accuracy and limits

This page says how far the numbers of Lunar Site Navigator can be trusted: what was checked and against what, what failed, what has not been checked at all, and which assumptions sit under every result. It is written for a reader who has to decide whether to rely on a number.

Built from 20 site packages and the stored validation results of the repository. Numbers are read from those records and are not recomputed here. The page needs no network connection and no login.

Not validated

  • Individual rise and set times on real terrain not validated
    The long-term shares of lit and Earth-visible time agree with the published LOLA maps on terrain of the same resolution; of the two checks against dated images one agrees and one fails its criterion; and no independent source gives the time of a sunrise or sunset at a point on real terrain. Where an event is set by terrain closer than about 1 km, the elevation grid limits its accuracy.
  • The elevation model itself not validated here
    Every terrain check on this card uses LOLA elevation data on both sides. The data stream checks the georeferencing of the LOLA files separately.
  • Exact rise and set times at a real lander not available
    No source examined for Blue Ghost Mission 1, Chandrayaan-3, SLIM, IM-1 or IM-2 prints a sunrise or sunset clock time for the landing site. Operational records (hours of daylight, expected wake-up date) only bound the computed events from one side.
  • Direct-to-Earth windows geometry validated, windows not
    Both ends of the link geometry agree with Horizons. The windows built from them, the station elevation limits as single numbers (no azimuth-dependent masks), and anything about link budget, multipath or scheduling have not been compared with an external source.
  • Earth rise and set against an independent source not done
    Horizons does not state which point of the Earth's disc its rise and set refer to; only the Earth's direction and size are validated.
  • Sunlight during an eclipse partly modelled
    Eclipses of the Sun by the Earth are computed for an opaque Earth without atmosphere and checked against one published eclipse. The light that the Earth's atmosphere refracts into the shadow, and any measured power curve of a lander, are not modelled or compared.

Limits of the validation as a whole

  • Near the lunar poles the terrain horizon, not the ephemeris, sets the accuracy of an event time: a horizon error of 0.01 deg can move an event by many minutes. The terrain checks on this card test long-term shares and two dated images, not event times.
  • The external reference (Horizons) and the core use JPL ephemerides of the same family; an error common to both would not show.
  • Thresholds marked as written in the acceptance test were committed in the same commit as the code they test; the thresholds of the comparisons with external sources have commits of their own that predate the data or the computation, as listed above.
  • The lander and eclipse comparisons are not blind: earlier research had compared some of the published values with Horizons.
  • Row 14 is not blind for one of its points. The horizon of the core was changed after this check had first failed (266 of 298 judged points, 89.3 %) and after the strip point row+700_along11500 had been examined: since contract 0.12 the ground between the site and the first pixel is in the horizon of an observer at 0 m. For that point the comparison is therefore no longer blind. It is the one strip point whose state changed with the new horizon (sunlit to shadow); the reading rule and the criterion are unchanged.
  • Rows 8 to 12 were recomputed on 15 November 2026 with the horizon the packages use, in which the ground between the point and the first pixel counts for an observer at 0 m. That this default is the one judged, and not the convention of pixel-based maps, was decided before the result was seen; the thresholds were not changed. The first result, of 14 November, searched from one pixel outward (Sun correlation 0.9973, permanent-shadow agreement 100 %).

Event times are approximate

A rise or set time in a package is the instant at which the computed margin between the body and the computed terrain horizon crosses zero. The arithmetic resolves that instant to a fraction of a second, but the terrain does not: no rise or set time on real terrain has been compared with an independent source, and near the poles a horizon error of 0.01° can move an event by many minutes. Treat event times as estimates with the band below where a package has one, and as estimates without a stated error where it has none.

What the band is. The terrain point that sets the horizon in each azimuth may be higher or lower than the elevation model says by the stated height; seen from the site that is atan(height error / distance), so near terrain gets a wide band and far terrain a narrow one. The same sign is assumed at every azimuth. The height is taken from the stated model: Two sigma for the LOLA products: on the 5 m site rasters twice the producers' RMS error map at the pixel (1 m where the map is empty); on every other product 5 m up to 30 degrees of slope and 7 m above. 'Certain' holds even with the horizon that much higher than computed; 'possible' needs it that much lower. Other error sources (ephemeris, landing position, observer height) are not in this band.

Assumptions behind every number

  • The Moon is described in the frame MOON_ME_DE440_ME421 on a reference sphere of 1737.4 km; heights are above that sphere.
  • Directions come from SPICE with aberration correction LT+S (light time and stellar aberration), kernel set bfe870fccfa5.
  • The Sun and the Earth are uniform discs. "Visible" means that any part of the disc is above the terrain horizon; limb darkening and the flattening of the Earth are not modelled.
  • The published window runs from 2026-11-14T00:00:00.000Z to 2028-11-14T00:00:00.000Z UTC on a grid of 3600 s; event times are found between the grid points. Outside the window nothing is published.
  • The terrain horizon is searched out to 300 km in every direction, for observer heights of 0, 2, 10 m above the terrain.
  • The observer is one point. A real lander has a footprint, a tilt and solar panels at several heights.
  • The Earth is opaque and has no atmosphere in the eclipse computation; light refracted into the shadow is not modelled.
  • Direct-to-Earth windows are geometry only: one elevation limit per ground station, no link budget, multipath or scheduling.
  • The landing requirement offered in the views is an example chosen by the team, not a mission requirement.
  • The sites are the project's own points or published landing coordinates. They are not official landing sites of a future mission.
